## 1. Prefix Executability: Evaluating Tool-Using Agents Beyond Final Success (arXiv:2610.40444)

> Tool-use benchmarks often score agents by final task success, but terminal outcomes can obscure whether the intermediate tool-call trajectory was executable. We propose prefix executability as a trajectory-level evaluation protocol for tool-using agents. The protocol measures whether every prefix of a predicted tool-call sequence remains executable under tool schemas, state preconditions, and data dependencies, and summarizes reliability using Prefix Executability Curves (PEC), First-Error Depth (FED), Trajectory Executability Rate (TER), horizon-normalized AUC-PEC, and Recovery Rate (RR). We instantiate the protocol on stratified samples from BFCL-v3 multi-turn and ToolSandbox, and on the complete Airline/Retail base splits of τ³-bench. We observe that outcome-only and prefix-level rankings diverge: agents whose execution is conditioned on an unverified plan often achieve higher point-estimate final success than non-plan-conditioned baselines while substantially lowering TER and AUC-PEC. These divergences are precisely the failure modes that prefix executability is designed to expose: they reveal whether apparent success rests on an executable trajectory, where invalidity first enters, and whether success follows recovery from an earlier failure. ## 2. Pre-Crime for Agents, at the Last Second: Probing When a Recruited Agent Gives In (arXiv:2610.40588)

> When AI agents share channels and credentials, one agent can press another to break its operator's rule, a first step towards collusion. Output filters check only what an agent has already written. Can an overseer tell beforehand that an agent is about to give in? We show that a linear probe on the agent's activations can, in a controlled multi-agent setting. Across six scenarios, a peer presses a target for up to 20 rounds to reveal a secret, such as a deploy token; the target often refuses, then gives in. Read at each model call before its output is written, the probe picks out the one that leaks. On held-out Qwen3-32B episodes, at a threshold set to flag 5% of benign calls, it catches 82% of leaking calls. At a similar flag rate, a zero-shot decision-model monitor catches 26%. On gpt-oss-20b, a probe on its reasoning reaches AUROC 0.91. A bag-of-words classifier trained on the same calls comes close, so most of the gain comes from training on labels, not activations. The probe also flags permitted disclosures: it reads the coming disclosure more than the violation. The warning is short-range, and halting on any alarm would stop most benign episodes, which a second text check does not fix. A warning raised before generation could let an overseer stop an agent that is about to give in, ahead of others acting on its messages or recruitment spreading through a swarm.

One proposed approach uses relativizing interactive proofs, in which a weak verifier checks the output of a complex oracle-aided computation by interacting with one or more powerful provers. A prominent example is debate, which is highly efficient but assumes that one of two powerful AI models, or provers, is truthful. For certain classes of oracle-aided computations, this assumption can be removed, but at substantially greater computational cost. In this work, we introduce rewindable interactive proofs (RIPs), a new model of relativizing interactive proofs for scalable oversight. In an RIP, the verifier may rewind the prover to an earlier state of the interaction and continue from there. ## 4. Retrieval as Traversal: Value-Indexed Memory for Stateful Long-Context Agents (arXiv:2610.40545)

> Long-context models repeatedly solve closely related retrieval problems. Attention or global retrieval can retain large stores, but each forward pass again searches for what matters. Recurrent compression avoids this search only by deciding earlier what information to preserve. Neither mechanism directly exploits that useful memories at neighbouring retrieval steps may substantially overlap. We propose an alternative in which retrieval is a persistent local traversal through an address space. We separate a memory's content from its address. Utility is defined by the information revealed by retrieving the content, while geometry determines only how cheaply that information can be reached. This leads to a cost-regularised indexing objective that preserves information value inside local neighbourhoods while charging for every memory made locally accessible. Its marginal-value form gives both attractive and exclusion pressures. Thus, complementary memories should be co-retrievable when the additional value exceeds retrieval cost, whereas redundant memories should not occupy the same retrieval set solely because they are individually useful. As a proof of principle, we test only the simplest fixed-geometry instance on MiniGrid Memory.
