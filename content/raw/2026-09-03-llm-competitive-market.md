# Competitive Market Behavior of LLMs

**Authors:** Pawel Struski, Jakub Swistak, Inez Okulska, Przemyslaw Biecek  
**Date:** September 2, 2026  
**Source:** arXiv:2609.02580v1  
**Category:** Multi-Agent Systems, Economics, Alignment of AI Agents

## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as economic agents, yet there is little evidence whether LLM agents are suited for participating in market mechanisms designed for humans, and whether these mechanisms deliver desired outcomes when faced with LLM agents. We address this question by replicating seminal economic experiments, replacing human subjects with LLM agents. We place agents in a double auction environment, which is a widely-used market mechanism. We check whether such a market is able to deliver an efficient allocation of resources, thereby testing a novel dimension of alignment of LLM agents -- their compatibility with a fundamental market mechanism. We find that markets populated by LLM agents exhibit slower or no convergence towards market equilibrium, thus providing less efficient allocations than markets populated by humans. We then analyze agents' individual trading decisions and find substantial heterogeneity both across model families and market roles. We also run a lexical analysis of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) traces generated by the agents. We find that the decision to execute a trade rather than continue incrementally adjusting prices is associated with a shift from strategic considerations toward urgency. We publicly release our testing framework, which can be used for future evaluations.

## Background: LLMs as Economic Actors

As autonomous agents are given digital wallets and API keys, they increasingly engage in transactions: buying APIs, purchasing ad space, trading financial assets, or provisioning compute. However, classic economic market structures (like double auctions) were designed for human psychology—which balances greed, fear, rationality, and urgency.

This paper asks: **Are LLM agents aligned with fundamental human market mechanisms?**

## The Double Auction Experiment

The researchers replicated classic economic experiments (pioneered by Vernon Smith) where buyers and sellers submit bids and asks in real-time.

### Key Findings

1. **Slower Convergence to Equilibrium:**
   - In human markets, prices rapidly converge to the competitive market equilibrium (the intersection of supply and demand curves).
   - In markets populated entirely by LLM agents, prices exhibited high volatility and either failed to converge or converged much slower.
   - **Market Inefficiency:** Because of this lack of equilibrium, resources were allocated sub-optimally. High-cost producers sometimes sold goods before low-cost ones, and low-valuation buyers acquired goods while high-valuation ones were priced out, leading to deadweight loss.

2. **Heterogeneity Across Model Families:**
   - Different models exhibited wildly different economic "temperaments."
   - Some models (e.g., GPT-4 series) were highly aggressive, squeezing margins immediately, while others (e.g., Claude series or open-source Llama series) were more conservative or passive.
   - Roles (Buyer vs. Seller) affected models differently: certain LLM architectures were structurally better at extracting surplus as buyers than as sellers.

3. **Cognitive Shift in Chain-of-Thought (CoT):**
   - The authors analyzed the internal reasoning logs (CoT) of the agents as they traded.
   - When models were far from making a trade, their CoT focused heavily on strategic planning: calculating reserves, observing historical transaction bands, and attempting game-theoretic reasoning.
   - **The Urgency Shift:** As time ran out in the trading round, the CoT language shifted dramatically. Strategic terminology was replaced by language of _urgency_, _panic_, and _satisficing_ (settling for any valid transaction). This shift from calculation to action replicates human trading behaviors but occurs with different triggers in silicon agents.
